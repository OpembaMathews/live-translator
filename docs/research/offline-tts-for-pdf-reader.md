# Layer neural voices over proven Windows speech

The best offline, PyTorch-free way to make this PDF reader sound natural is two engines: **Kokoro-82M through ONNX Runtime for English**, and **MediaTek's Breeze2-VITS-onnx through sherpa-onnx for Taiwan Mandarin**. The Windows SAPI voices the app already uses (Zira and Hanhan) should stay in the app as the always-available fallback, not be replaced. Kokoro is the only small open model with a real naturalness track record: about **Elo 1056 on TTS Arena V2, ahead of 0.5B–7B models such as Chatterbox, VibeVoice 7B and XTTS-v2**. Its model also predicts a duration for every phoneme, so exact word highlighting comes free. Its Mandarin voices are weak, though, and its accent is mainland. Breeze2-VITS is the only open, ONNX-ready Taiwan-accented voice found anywhere. It is a single 121 MB file distilled from MediaTek's BreezyVoice. Its licence is not stated, it has not been updated in over a year, and it gives no timing data. Every heavier model fails at least one hard constraint. XTTS-v2, F5, ChatTTS, Spark and Fish carry non-commercial weights. BreezyVoice, IndexTTS, VibeVoice, Bark, StyleTTS2 and Parler depend on PyTorch in practice. CosyVoice3, the one heavy model that is permissive and PyTorch-free, has no published CPU speed and no timing data. Three risks come before any of this: the espeak-ng DLL may be blocked by Smart App Control, no engine has a measured speed on an x86 laptop CPU, and the Breeze2 licence is unconfirmed. Test all three on the Ryzen 7840HS before writing integration code. If offline quality is not good enough, Azure is the clear cloud fallback: it returns word boundaries in the same call and costs about **$0.60 per 40,000-character paper**.

## Kokoro wins English on quality, size and timing; nothing small wins Mandarin

Most models in the survey fail a hard constraint before sound quality even matters, so this section sorts candidates by what disqualifies them. The only open-weight model above Kokoro on the Arena is Fish Audio S2 Pro (Elo 1128.7), and its weights are under the non-commercial Fish Audio Research License. On a board of 74 models, the permissive models ranked below Kokoro include Chatterbox (1006.4), VibeVoice 7B (959.7), XTTS v2 (885.9) and StyleTTS 2 (878.8) ([offlinetts.com](https://offlinetts.com/blog/tts-arena-leaderboard-2026/)). A July 2026 update put Kokoro at rank 48. Its Elo in the "knowledge sharing" category, the one closest to reading papers aloud, was 1065.8 ([offlinetts.com](https://offlinetts.com/blog/tts-arena-leaderboard-2026/)). Arena votes come from short English clips, so these figures say nothing about Mandarin or about 40 minutes of continuous listening. No long-form listening study exists for any engine in scope.

Kokoro's strength is English only. The model card grades **af_heart A and af_bella A-**. It grades **all eight Mandarin voices in v1.0 D**, trained on 1–10 hours of Chinese in total ([Kokoro VOICES.md](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M/blob/main/VOICES.md)). The separate Kokoro-82M-v1.1-zh, trained on more than 100 hours from 100 professional speakers, is the only Kokoro build worth testing for Chinese. Its own author says it was "released early to gather feedback" ([hexgrad/Kokoro-82M-v1.1-zh](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M-v1.1-zh)). The weights are Apache-2.0, and kokoro-onnx is MIT. Model files come in 326 MB (fp32), 164 MB (fp16) and 114 MB (int8) versions ([kokoro-onnx releases](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/releases)).

The other lightweight engines are more robotic or narrower. **Piper (now piper1-gpl, v1.8.0, September 2026)** is actively maintained. It ships as a single Windows wheel with espeak-ng built in, supports Python 3.9–3.13 ([PyPI piper-tts](https://pypi.org/project/piper-tts/)), and from v1.6.1 uses an ONNX g2pW Chinese phonemizer that removed a ~750 MB torch dependency ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Its Chinese voices are all zh_CN, though. One of them, huayan, lists its dataset licence as "Unknown" ([huayan MODEL_CARD](https://huggingface.co/rhasspy/piper-voices/blob/main/zh/zh_CN/huayan/medium/MODEL_CARD)). **Sherpa-ONNX** is best understood as a runtime, not a voice. It runs Kokoro, Piper/VITS, Matcha, Kitten, MMS, MeloTTS (vits-melo-tts-zh_en) and ZipVoice, and it ships Chinese normalisation FSTs for dates, numbers and phone numbers ([sherpa-onnx models](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/tts/pretrained_models/index.html)). It is the most active project in the field, with weekly releases up to v1.13.8 on 2026-09-10 ([releases](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/releases)). Its Chinese voices other than Kokoro are mainland corpora such as Baker and AISHELL-3. **Matcha-TTS** reaches the app only as sherpa's single-speaker matcha-icefall-zh-baker. **MeloTTS** is MIT-licensed and its Chinese speaker handles mixed Chinese and English ([MeloTTS README](https://github.com/myshell-ai/MeloTTS/blob/main/README.md)), but it is mainland-accented and absent from the Arena snapshot. **Kitten TTS** is English-only, and its README warns about the nano int8 model ([KittenTTS](https://github.com/KittenML/KittenTTS)). **MMS-TTS** fails on three counts. Its weights are **CC-BY-NC 4.0**, its Hugging Face path needs PyTorch, and its stochastic duration predictor makes each run sound different unless a seed is fixed ([facebook/mms-tts-eng](https://huggingface.co/facebook/mms-tts-eng)).

CosyVoice3 deserves a steelman. It is Apache-2.0, bilingual, reports a **1.21% Chinese CER**, and offers pinyin and CMU-phoneme "pronunciation inpainting" to fix mispronounced jargon ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/FunAudioLLM/Fun-CosyVoice3-0.5B-2512)). If cosyvoice.cpp's Vulkan backend turned out to run fast on the Radeon 780M iGPU, it could become the quality option. Reliability rules it out as the primary engine for now. Its hard-text CER works out to roughly one error every 15–20 characters on the prose academic papers contain. No codec-LM model in scope has documented stable 40-minute reading. And none exposes durations, so highlighting would need a second model. Treat it as an experiment, not a foundation.

## Taiwan Mandarin narrows the field to one open model and the built-in voices

Taiwanese accent and Traditional-character handling exclude almost everything. The CosyVoice base model "predominantly associates Traditional Chinese characters with Cantonese". MediaTek Research built **BreezyVoice** (Apache-2.0) because mainland Mandarin differs lexically and phonologically from Taiwan Mandarin ([BreezyVoice paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17790)). BreezyVoice uses g2pW for polyphones: in 23 hard cases, g2pW corrected all but one of the 8 errors the pipeline made without it. It won preference tests against four anonymised commercial systems, though its **SSL-MOS of 4.46 did not beat the best competitor's 4.63** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17790)). It is a CosyVoice-derived LLM-plus-flow pipeline with Python 3.10 tooling ([GitHub](https://github.com/mtkresearch/BreezyVoice)). That rules it out at runtime under the no-PyTorch constraint, but makes it a good quality reference.

Its distilled offspring, **Breeze2-VITS-onnx**, is the practical answer. It is a zh-TW VITS model "built upon Sherpa" and "distilled from BreezyVoice to run on mobile devices". It consists of breeze2-vits.onnx (121 MB), a 2.52 MB lexicon and a tokens file. It has had five commits, the last "over 1 year ago", and **no licence tag** ([HF file tree](https://huggingface.co/MediaTek-Research/Breeze2-VITS-onnx/tree/main)). BreezyVoice's Apache-2.0 licence does not automatically cover the distilled model. Before redistributing it in a public repo, get written confirmation from MediaTek, or download the model at first run from Hugging Face instead of bundling it. Three more cautions apply. The lexicon front-end probably lacks g2pW-level polyphone resolution, its handling of English terms inside Chinese text is unknown, and no published MOS or speed figures exist.

The fallback options are mainland-accented, and each needs Traditional characters converted to Simplified with OpenCC first. They are Kokoro v1.1-zh, Piper zh_CN, Matcha-baker and MeloTTS-zh_en. Conversion fixes the characters but not the readings: 垃圾, 和 and neutral tones will still come out the mainland way. No survey of Taiwanese students' tolerance for mainland TTS was found.

The Windows baseline is stronger than it looks, because it already works. A local registry check found **Hanhan (zh-TW) and Huihui exposed as SAPI5 Desktop voices**. It also found **Yating and Zhiwei (zh-TW) available only as OneCore voices** through WinRT `Windows.Media.SpeechSynthesis`, whose `IncludeWordBoundaryMetadata` and `IncludeSentenceBoundaryMetadata` options supply timing cues ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/windows.media.speechsynthesis.speechsynthesizeroptions.includewordboundarymetadata)). Adding Yating through WinRT would give students a second Taiwan voice at no install cost. The Windows 11 Narrator "natural" voices are off-limits: they are exclusive to Narrator ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_text-to-speech_voices)). The only bridge to them, NaturalVoiceSAPIAdapter, uses "encryption keys extracted from system files". Its own wiki says the current Store packages broke when "the decryption key was changed", and it offers no zh-TW voice ([README](https://github.com/gexgd0419/NaturalVoiceSAPIAdapter/blob/master/README.md), [wiki](https://github.com/gexgd0419/NaturalVoiceSAPIAdapter/wiki/Narrator-natural-voice-download-links)).

## Word timing comes from model durations, then proportional estimates, then alignment

Only one candidate gives exact word times natively. **Kokoro's ONNX graph outputs `pred_dur`**, the per-phoneme durations in decoder frames. To get word boundaries, split the phonemised string on spaces and sum each word's durations ([audio.cpp PR #626](https://github.com/0xShug0/audio.cpp/pull/626)). The plain `kokoro-v1.0.onnx` returns audio only. The durations come from the kokoro-onnx v1.1 model files, which "added duration output functionality for timestamps" ([kokoro-onnx releases](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/releases)), or from the separate timestamped re-export ([onnx-community discussion](https://huggingface.co/onnx-community/Kokoro-82M-v1.0-ONNX-timestamped/discussions/2)). One reported bug needs guarding against. kokoro-onnx looks for an output named `duration` while the export names it `durations`, so `has_timings` stays false and no error is raised ([reader #16](https://github.com/HyperToken9/reader/issues/16)). It is unconfirmed against current source. The safest integration is to open the ONNX session yourself and assert that the duration tensor is present at startup.

**Piper** exposes per-phoneme sample counts through an experimental alignment feature. Install `piper-tts[alignment]`, then patch the voice or load it with `include_alignments=True`. `AudioChunk` then carries `phoneme_alignments`, and the array is empty if the voice cannot support it ([ALIGNMENTS.md](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/docs/ALIGNMENTS.md)). Mapping phonemes back to words is left to the app. **Sherpa-ONNX exposes no TTS timestamps.** Request #3705 (June 2026) has no maintainer reply ([sherpa-onnx #3705](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/issues/3705)). That applies to Breeze2-VITS. Calibre, facing the same gap with Piper, settled for sentence highlighting ([Calibre manual](https://manual.calibre-ebook.com/viewer.html), [linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/calibre-7-21-ebook-management-tool/)).

For engines without durations, a three-level fallback works. First, synthesise one sentence at a time, which gives exact sentence timing for free. Second, spread each sentence's audio duration across its characters and merge the spans into words ([Ryan Welch](https://ryanwelch.co.uk/blog/kokoro-word-timestamps/)). This keeps any error inside one sentence. It should work better for Mandarin than for English, because each Hanzi is one syllable, while English syllables per letter vary widely. That is an inference and needs checking by ear. Third, optionally realign after synthesis. The app already ships **faster-whisper, whose `word_timestamps=True` uses DTW over cross-attention**, but Whisper word-timing evaluations typically allow a 200 ms collar ([CrisperWhisper paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16589)). Inside one short sentence, that is barely better than proportional estimates. `ctc-forced-aligner` runs on ONNX Runtime, but its default MMS_FA model is **CC-BY-NC 4.0** ([deskpai/ctc_forced_aligner](https://github.com/deskpai/ctc_forced_aligner)). A forced aligner is therefore not worth the added model for this app.

Perception research sets the error budget. Asynchrony is detectable at about **+45 ms when audio leads and −125 ms when it lags** ([testdevlab on ITU BT.1359](https://www.testdevlab.com/blog/how-to-test-audio-video-sync)). The tolerance is asymmetric in favour of late visuals ([PMC3314884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3314884/)). These are lip-sync studies. No study specific to read-along highlighting was found. In practice, schedule the highlight a few tens of milliseconds late rather than early. Also drive it from the audio device's playback position, not from the synthesis clock: Azure's own docs warn that boundary events "arrive faster than real-time playback" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/how-to-speech-synthesis)).

## Smart App Control and espeak-ng are the reliability risks, not the neural models

The model files are small and the RAM budget is generous. The largest candidate file is about 330 MB, well under 7.2 GB. Speed is plausible but unmeasured. The only published figures for Kokoro multi-lang are on a Raspberry Pi 4: **RTF 3.19 on 4 threads**, meaning slower than real time ([sherpa-onnx Kokoro docs](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/tts/pretrained_models/kokoro.html)). A Zen 4 laptop should be several times faster, but no x86 laptop measurement exists for any engine. At a hypothetical RTF of 0.3, a 40-minute paper takes about 12 minutes of compute. The app must therefore synthesise a few sentences ahead of playback, never the whole paper up front.

Packaging is the more fragile part. The `espeakng-loader` wheel that kokoro-onnx uses has a **data path hard-coded at CI build time**, and kokoro-onnx ignores `ESPEAK_DATA_PATH` ([kokoro-onnx #175](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/issues/175), [#120](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/issues/120)). Mismatched misaki/phonemizer versions break with `'EspeakWrapper' has no attribute 'set_data_path'` ([#111](https://github.com/thewh1teagle/kokoro-onnx/issues/111)). A frozen build must bundle `espeak-ng-data` and set both the library and the data path before creating the engine. Smart App Control is the bigger threat. It blocks unsigned DLLs and `.pyd` files that PyInstaller extracts, with **no "Run anyway" option** ([pyinstaller #6747](https://github.com/pyinstaller/pyinstaller/issues/6747), [note.com](https://note.com/liuk_net/n/n5b11e4018dfc?hl=en)). In one reported case, an unsigned `_sqlite3.pyd` stopped an app's backend from starting ([hermes-agent #89627](https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent/issues/89627)). Even signed apps get blocked sometimes ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5791210/application-signed-and-blocked-by-smart-app-contro)). An unsigned `espeak-ng.dll` is the most likely new block. Build with `--onedir` so files are not re-extracted to %TEMP% on every launch.

That risk shapes the architecture. Whichever neural engine is in use, the reader must detect at startup that it can load. Every failure — a blocked DLL, a missing duration tensor, a synthesis exception, or a sentence whose audio length is implausible for its text — must drop to the SAPI/WinRT voice for that sentence without stopping playback. A student should hear a less natural voice, never silence.

## Licences mostly suit an open-source release; commercial use has three snags

A public open-source project can use everything recommended here, with care. The commercial picture has three snags. First, **piper1-gpl switched to GPL-3.0-or-later when it embedded espeak-ng** ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Its MIT predecessor was archived on 2025-10-06 ([rhasspy/piper](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper)). Any engine that ships espeak-ng for English phonemes carries the same copyleft question, and that includes kokoro-onnx. A GPL app is fine; a closed-source commercial release is not, unless English G2P moves to a lexicon such as misaki's with espeak-ng removed. Second, **Breeze2-VITS has no stated licence**. Third, Kokoro's Apache-2.0 weights were partly trained on "synthetic audio from closed TTS systems" ([Kokoro-82M card](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M)), a provenance question for commercial use rather than a legal bar. The hard blockers are XTTS-v2, F5, Spark, ChatTTS, Fish S2, Higgs v3, MMS-TTS and the MMS aligner, all non-commercial. IndexTTS's terms conflict between sources.

## Conclusion

This decision turns less on which open model sounds best than on which failure a student can survive. The strongest open voices by Arena score are either non-commercial or tied to PyTorch. The two engines that pass every constraint are small (82M parameters and 121 MB). Their real risks are packaging (espeak-ng under Smart App Control), missing metadata (sherpa-onnx has no timestamps), and paperwork (Breeze2's licence), not model quality. That argues for treating the SAPI path the app already has as core infrastructure: a per-sentence safety net under a neural voice that can be swapped out.

Two developments would change this ranking. If sherpa-onnx adds `return_timestamps` (#3705), a single runtime could drive both Kokoro and Breeze2 with exact word timing, and kokoro-onnx's espeak packaging could be dropped. If a measured CPU or Vulkan benchmark shows cosyvoice.cpp running faster than real time on the 780M, a permissive bilingual model with pronunciation control would become a real contender for the Mandarin slot. It would still need an alignment layer and hallucination checks. Until either happens, Taiwan Mandarin quality offline is capped at a distilled VITS model, and Azure HsiaoChen remains the benchmark for what students will compare it against.
